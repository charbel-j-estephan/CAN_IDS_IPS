Stopping car network attacks while they are still on the wire

One random forest on an FPGA and on a microcontroller, destroying attack frames before any computer in the car accepts them, with a secure way to update the model.

What is CAN, and why is it open to anyone?

CAN: the network inside every car

Two wires. Dozens of small computers. Every message goes to everyone.

Why CAN is not protected

It was designed in the 1980s for a closed box. Today the box has a modem, Bluetooth and an open port.

No sender

A frame says what it is, never who sent it.

No signature

Nothing proves a frame is genuine.

No secrecy

Every node hears every frame.

Lowest ID wins

Anyone can claim top priority.

Four attacks from a real car (Car Hacking Dataset)

Recorded on a real car's bus by the Hacking and Countermeasure Research Lab. We replay them on our bench.

DoS

Floods the bus with the highest-priority ID. Real messages cannot get through.

Fuzzy

Sprays random messages to find one that makes the car react.

Gear spoofing

Uses the gearbox's own ID with false data.

RPM spoofing

Uses the engine's own ID with false speed values.

Spoofing is the hard one: the ID is genuine, so a list of allowed IDs cannot catch it. Only the data gives it away.

IDS raises an alarm. IPS stops the frame.

The difference is when: after the frame, or while it is still on the wire.

Where CAN runs, and needs an IDS/IPS

CAN is not only in cars. Anywhere a CAN bus can be reached from outside, it can be attacked.

Passenger cars

OBD-II dongles, telematics, infotainment. In 2015 a Jeep Cherokee was controlled remotely through its radio; 1.4 million vehicles were recalled.

Trucks and buses

SAE J1939 on CAN: engine, brakes, fleet trackers plugged into the bus.

Farm and construction machines

ISOBUS (ISO 11783): tractors and implements from different makers share one bus.

Factories and robots

CANopen links drives, sensors and controllers on production lines.

Elevators and buildings

CANopen-Lift connects elevator controllers, doors and call panels.

Ships

NMEA 2000 (built on CAN) carries engine, GPS and autopilot data.

Aircraft

ARINC 825 uses CAN for avionics subsystems.

Electric vehicles

Battery management, motor control and fast charging (CHAdeMO talks over CAN).

For cars it is now the law: UN Regulation 155 makes new vehicles in the EU detect and respond to cyberattacks, with ISO/SAE 21434 as the engineering standard.

One model, three detectors, a real bus

One random forest, the same everywhere

5 decision trees, 9 levels deep. Each tree asks yes/no questions about the frame; the majority vote decides.

Verilog, C and Python agree on every distinct frame of the dataset (675,167 frames, 0 differ).

The basic IDS: the forest as pure logic

Each question becomes a comparator

The trees are compiled into the FPGA's logic: every node is a small circuit, and all five trees answer in the same clock.

Why an FPGA

The window shrinks as the model reads more bytes. The FPGA's decision time does not move.

On the bench (11-03) the basic FPGA with 5, 6 and 8 bytes destroyed every attack frame in all four attacks.

Why we made the forest modular

The problem with the basic IDS

A new attack appears. With the trees compiled into logic, changing the model means:

The idea: a fixed engine, the model as data

Like a processor and its program. The circuit never changes; the trees are numbers in memory.

When does a real vehicle need a modular forest?

A new attack is found

Retrain on it and send the new model to the whole fleet, the way phones get security updates.

A software update in the car

A new ECU firmware changes which IDs and values are normal: the model must follow.

One chip, many vehicles

The same board in different models and trims, each with its own traffic and its own trees.

Too many false alarms

Fix a model that blocks a real frame without touching the hardware.

The law asks for it

UN R155 expects makers to respond to new threats for the whole life of the vehicle.

More features later

Read 6 or 8 bytes instead of 5: the same engine takes the larger model.

And the detector never stops: the new model loads into the spare bank and takes over between two frames.

An update port is also a door

A fake model could tell the detector that the attack is normal. So who may change the trees?

The path of an update

The PC signs. The STM32 only carries the file. The FPGA checks and decides.

Honest limits

Not done yet (production steps)

The board's key is inside the FPGA design file. A product would program it encrypted, with JTAG secure and verify protect turned on, so the key cannot be read back. We did not do this on our board.

Bench, not a car

500 kbit/s, four nodes, short wires. The bench replays the first 5,000 or 20,000 frames of each file; accuracy on unseen frames comes from simulation (6.5 million frames).

A few lost normal frames

The STM32 lost the same 3 normal RPM frames in every 20,000-frame run. The FPGA lost 1 normal frame in one DoS rehearsal run (0 in every cited run). Not yet explained.

Only the 5-byte model on the bench for the updatable FPGA

6 and 8 bytes on the updatable design were checked in simulation only.
